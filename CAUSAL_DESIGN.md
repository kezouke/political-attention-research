# From crisis to trust and votes: a testable design

## Priority and feasibility

The first credible target is the **total local effect of Spain's construction collapse on subsequent turnout and party vote shares**. Municipal election returns, pre-2008 construction intensity, local unemployment and housing indicators can in principle be assembled without a new survey. This would not identify a trust pathway. A stronger claim, that job loss changed trust and trust then changed a person's vote, needs linked individual observations in the right order. The current ESS waves and CIS snapshots do not provide that sequence. A new panel or a suitable restricted panel would be needed, with pre-crisis party attachment, subsequent income or job loss, later institutional trust and an observed or validated post-crisis vote.

The 2021–2022 price shock is the second target because the same broad Eurobarometer question covers Spain, Bulgaria and Serbia. The 16-country comparison in this repository found higher price salience everywhere but no positive cross-country dose-response to the change in annual inflation. That result says the simple national inflation gradient is insufficient. It does not select between household losses, wage adjustment, government cushioning, fieldwork dates or political messages. The feasible next step is a within-country design with household energy expenditure before the shock, actual retail tariffs and transfers during it, repeated priority questions and later votes.

| Link to test | Outcome and data | Design that could identify more | Main failure condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure to loss | Employment or real disposable income by person or locality, measured after pre-shock construction or energy dependence | Compare more and less exposed units around one shock; verify pre-trends and a first-stage income loss | Exposure predicts no differential loss, or outcomes diverge before the shock |
| Loss to trust | Repeated 0–10 trust in parliament or government, with the same respondent's earlier trust and party identification | Individual panel or linked locality cohorts with trust measured after loss and before election | Trust falls before loss, or the association disappears with earlier trust and stable traits |
| Trust to vote | Validated party choice, switching and turnout at a later election | Panel transitions with baseline vote; estimate the total shock effect first, then test mediation with explicit assumptions | Vote shifts despite unchanged trust, or trust shifts without later switching |
| Political supply | Dated advertisements, news, reach and audience media use before survey fieldwork | Compare otherwise similar audiences facing plausibly external variation in message exposure; control prior concern | Messages follow concern or differential reach does not predict later change |
| Policy buffering | Actual household transfers, tax and energy bills, including who funds them | Test whether measured compensation changes the exposure effect, not merely whether a country announced support | Compensation reaches exposed households but does not modify losses or concern within precision |

Neither a municipality-level vote effect nor a trust coefficient is by itself proof that trust mediates voting. Even a panel can leave unmeasured anger, ideology or family changes affecting trust and vote together. The analysis should publish the total effect, each intermediate association, plausible alternative pathways and sensitivity to unmeasured confounding separately. An instrument for job loss would not automatically identify the trust-to-vote link because job loss can change votes through income, anger or migration without changing trust.

## What the current evidence establishes

The ESS analysis measures financial difficulty and trust in the same interview. It finds an adjusted difference of −0.68 points on a 0–10 parliamentary-trust scale across 22 countries. It does not establish which came first or whether either changed a vote. The 14-country unemployment regression compares country-years, not people. Neither is a mediation test. The Spanish CIS series measures problem rankings, not party choice.

We would test three rival mechanisms. **Material exposure:** regions hit harder by a common shock change priorities and votes more, even if pre-shock political supply is similar. **Institutional buffering:** comparable losses have smaller political effects where transfers, public services or prior trust cushion the hit. **Political supply:** under comparable losses, parties' messages and the media people actually use predict which issue becomes salient. Each mechanism permits the others; the design aims to estimate where one adds explanatory power and to find cases that contradict it.

## Spain's construction collapse as the first case

The relevant unit is a municipality or commuting zone observed before and after the 2008 shock, ideally joined to repeated individual interviews. Measure pre-crisis construction employment, mortgage and credit exposure before 2007. Then measure subsequent job losses, household income or benefit receipt, trust, turnout and vote shares for established and new parties at each election. Municipal returns come from the Spanish Interior Ministry; employment histories may be studied through the [Spanish Social Security MCVL](https://portaldatos.seg-social.gob.es/en/mcvl). The MCVL contains work and income histories but no trust or vote; those must come from surveys or locality-level outcomes. The [CIS 2015 election panel](https://www.cis.es/documents/d/guest/IM7715pdfpdf) follows respondents across the election, but starts years after the crash and cannot by itself measure pre-crisis trust. ESS party-vote and trust items are retrospective or contemporaneous, not a causal panel.

Pre-register event time, the exposure index, geographical unit, party classification and the main outcomes. First show that the shock actually reduced jobs or income more in exposed places. Compare exposed and less exposed places before 2008 for unemployment, turnout, party support, age structure and housing prices. Plot coefficients for every pre- and post-crisis year rather than reporting only one before/after difference. Include region-by-year factors, baseline urbanisation and industry composition, and report how migration changes the population being compared. A failed pre-trend test or large differential migration would weaken the design.

An exposure-by-post difference-in-differences estimate still needs a credible counterfactual: absent the crash, high- and low-construction places would have followed parallel political paths. Construction intensity can also proxy for migrant share, housing wealth or urban growth. A bank-credit instrument would require a separate argument that bank weakness changed voting only through local economic exposure, not through bank bailouts or other political channels. An existing study, [When Credit Turns Political](https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.875513.de/publikationen/diskussionspapiere/2023_2042/when_credit_turns_political__evidence_from_the_spanish_financial_crisis.html), links weak-bank exposure, firms, employment and city-level electoral outcomes. It is a benchmark to replicate and challenge, not a substitute for an independently validated trust pathway.

[Algan and co-authors' 2017 European study](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/algantextfa17bpea.pdf) uses pre-crisis construction intensity as an instrument for regional unemployment. The authors discuss possible direct links from construction to voting through corruption, immigration and human capital. Published discussants also note that the paper's trust and vote analyses run in parallel rather than identifying a trust-mediated effect. A Spanish follow-up should therefore report the construction exposure's total effect on voting and trust separately; it should not label their overlap a mediated effect. Check baseline migrant composition, housing wealth and local development before interpreting the construction instrument.

## The trust pathway requires more than a trust coefficient

For a mediation claim, exposure must precede trust measurement, and trust must precede the measured vote or intention. One needs repeated respondents or a carefully linked sequence of comparable local surveys and election returns. Measure pre-shock trust and party identification to distinguish a changed attitude from stable differences between people. Record media use and party messaging before the vote. Track attrition and survey-mode changes. If job loss predicts trust but trust does not predict later switching after prior partisanship, the proposed mediation fails. If the exposure predicts votes while trust is unchanged, another pathway is needed. Even a temporal sequence does not prove mediation: unmeasured anger, ideology or local services may affect both trust and voting. A causal mediation estimate would need much stronger assumptions or a separate source of variation in trust.

## A second common shock and cross-country comparison

For 2021–2022 energy prices, use pre-shock household fuel and electricity spending by income group, regional industry energy intensity and the exposure of import-dependent countries. Observe actual retail tariffs, wage growth, subsidies and transfers, then survey priorities and election outcomes at dates after those changes. Compare like-for-like survey questions and elections, with timing made explicit. Spain, Bulgaria and Serbia have some comparable priority questions; Brazil, Israel and Thailand do not share that instrument. A pooled six-country causal coefficient would manufacture comparability. Country-specific designs can instead test whether exposure predicts price concern and whether policy buffering weakens that link. The test should allow a fossil-fuel exporter to face domestic inflation and a country with high clean-energy spending to retain climate concern.

For the political-supply hypothesis, collect dated party advertisements, speeches and news items with outlet reach, audience or broadcast frequency. Validate issue labels on a stratified human-coded sample and publish confusion matrices by language and issue. Model messages before and after survey fieldwork. Prior public concern must enter the model because parties respond to it. A message–concern correlation, even with a lag, is not a causal effect without a credible change in exposure independent of audience preferences.

An issue count in a party speech measures the speaker's strategic emphasis. [Guriev and Treisman](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.33.4.100) document performance language in leaders' speeches, while their theory gives rulers incentives to manage information. Speech frequency must therefore not be equated with public urgency. [Guriev, Melnikov and Zhuravskaya](https://www.n-melnikov.com/_files/ugd/87e482_4ed519a52ec84da59daf198da5eafff8.pdf) find that the political effect of 3G expansion varies with internet censorship, traditional-media control and corruption. A supply test should specify these moderators in advance and measure actual reach, rather than assume the same message effect everywhere. [Guriev and Papaioannou's review](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2656/1/Guriev%20Papaioannou.pdf) also describes cases of cultural mobilisation without a new economic shock. Include such cases as counterexamples to a shock-required model.

## Decision rules

The material account is weakened if exposed places show no differential income or employment loss, or if priorities diverge before the shock. Institutional buffering is weakened if measured transfers and prior trust do not modify the exposure–outcome relation under adequate power. Political supply is weakened if comparable constituencies with markedly different verified message exposure respond alike. Record null estimates with uncertainty, alternate exposure definitions and party classifications, and leave-one-region-out checks. A successful study may support one transition in the chain while leaving the others unresolved.
