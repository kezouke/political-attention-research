# Reading the political-economy sources

This note records what the cited works actually identify and what this project can borrow from them. It is not a claim that their authors have reviewed or endorsed our study.

## Algan, Guriev, Papaioannou and Passari (2017)

[The European Trust Crisis and the Rise of Populism](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/algantextfa17bpea.pdf) combines European regional election returns, unemployment and ESS attitudes around the Great Recession. Its within-region estimates link an increase in unemployment, rather than unemployment's level, to higher anti-establishment vote shares. An instrumental-variable design uses pre-crisis construction intensity to predict the unemployment shock. The authors report roughly 2–4 percentage points more anti-establishment vote for one percentage point more unemployment in those specifications. That causal reading requires construction intensity to affect votes through unemployment alone. The paper explicitly notes possible alternative paths through corruption, immigration or human capital. Its published discussants further question the construction instrument and observe that the paper estimates trust and voting outcomes in parallel, without identifying trust as the mediator.

The ESS portion is a useful counterexample to a mechanical hardship-to-cultural-hostility story. Regional unemployment is associated with the belief that immigration damages the economy, while the association with immigrants' cultural contribution is near zero and statistically insignificant in the relevant specifications (table 14). Left-right responses differ by country. This does not rule out cultural activation in other shocks or later periods. It does show that our individual cross-sectional association between subjective financial difficulty and a cultural-immigration attitude must not be presented as the same mechanism.

## Guriev and Papaioannou (2022)

[The Political Economy of Populism](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2656/1/Guriev%20Papaioannou.pdf) is a review, not a single causal estimate. It covers trade, automation, financial crises, austerity, culture, immigration, media and policy responses. It treats cultural persistence as an identification problem: a stable attitude cannot by itself explain the timing of a sudden political shift. The authors distinguish additive economic and cultural explanations from interaction, where an economic shock activates an existing divide. They give evidence for both and an Austrian case in which a party's historical-cultural framing gained traction without a contemporaneous economic shock. Their conclusion calls the activation hypothesis plausible but not yet rigorously established. They specifically identify the trust pathway, party supply and the role of social protection as open research questions. Our study should not claim these mechanisms as established results.

## Guriev, Melnikov and Zhuravskaya (2021)

[3G Internet and Confidence in Government](https://www.n-melnikov.com/_files/ugd/87e482_4ed519a52ec84da59daf198da5eafff8.pdf) analyzes 840,537 Gallup respondents in 2,232 regions of 116 countries during 2008–2017. A region's typical 39-percentage-point growth in 3G coverage corresponds in the authors' model to 2.5 percentage points less government confidence, from a 51% mean. Their design uses within-region rollout and lightning frequency as an instrument; it depends on rollout and lightning not changing confidence through omitted paths. Effects differ: the fall appears where the internet is uncensored, is stronger where traditional media are controlled, and reverses in the least corrupt settings. The paper's European election analysis finds incumbent losses and populist opposition gains, but it explicitly does not isolate why the populist opposition benefits. It measures connectivity, approval, corruption perceptions and election results, not which issues voters call urgent in our six countries or the effects of a recommendation algorithm. Its Brazil 2018 example is illustrative rather than a test that Bolsonaro's messages caused the earlier 2017 corruption peak in Latinobarómetro.

## Guriev and Treisman (2019, 2020)

[Informational Autocrats](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.33.4.100) combines descriptive data on authoritarian control techniques with an analysis of leaders' speeches. It reports that leaders classified as informational autocrats emphasize economic performance and services more than overt dictators do. The authors acknowledge that speech samples and English translation affect dictionary counts. Their [formal model](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2020.104158) gives rulers incentives to preserve a reputation for competence using propaganda, censorship and co-optation. The [full theoretical manuscript](https://www.danieltreisman.org/s/Guriev-Treisman-February-2020.pdf) makes the trade-off explicit: propaganda, censorship and co-optation cost resources that could otherwise support consumption, while an informed elite can disclose poor performance. Its regime predictions depend on the size of that elite and on the costs of controlling media; the authors call their media-ownership proxy imperfect. These are models and descriptive patterns about rule and political communication. A high share of economic words in a ruler's speeches does not establish a high share of public concern about the economy. Nor can their classification automatically be applied to a particular country-year in our sample.

## Movchan, Zotin and Grigoryev (2017)

[Managing the Resource Curse](https://carnegieendowment.org/russia-eurasia/posts/2017/03/managing-the-resource-curse-strategies-of-oil-dependent-economies-in-the-modern-era) is a comparative policy report on ten oil-dependent economies, written with lessons for Russia. Russia itself is not one of the ten cases. None of the six countries in Minaev's list is a case. The report emphasizes that resource abundance, openness to investment and technology, saving or spending rents, and protection of household incomes during reform can interact. It groups countries using oil output per person and oil output relative to GDP, while acknowledging that the latter also reflects other determinants of GDP. Its openness and diversification claim follows case comparison, not a design that identifies a causal coefficient. Our net energy import chart measures a different quantity and cannot test the resource-curse argument or rank countries' fiscal ability to cushion a price shock.

## Changes required in this project

1. Mark the trust-to-vote step as unestimated even when citing a paper with stronger identification of the unemployment-to-vote step.
2. Separate material changes in immigration attitudes from cultural changes, and test interaction rather than assume that hardship creates hostility.
3. Treat party speeches as a strategic signal of what elites want to emphasize, not as an observation of public priorities.
4. Measure actual household losses, compensation and usable fiscal resources before interpreting energy trade or government revenue as political exposure.
5. Use shock timing, pre-shock culture and message exposure to distinguish material, activation and political-supply accounts.
