# Spain: identity-adjacent national priorities and immigration

Housing is extracted from the same national top-three question as a competing material priority. Its September values in 2023–2026 are 6.4%, 15.4%, 30.4% and 37.5%. Immigration declines from its 2024 peak of 30.4% to 20.7% and 19.7% in 2025 and 2026. The article chart focuses on these four telephone waves; questionnaire position and recoding still differ. The Bank of Spain's 2024 annual report provides an independent national affordability benchmark, not respondent-level evidence of why housing was named.

`python analysis/14_identity_spain_cis.py --download` fetches eight original CIS September barometers and extracts their published percentages. Run without `--download` if the PDFs are already in `data/raw/`. PDF links, hashes, field dates, interview counts, survey modes, labels and source pages are in `data/processed/identity_spain_cis_selected.csv`. Dates, counts and modes were manually transcribed, not independently verified by code. The source PDF, not a secondary quotation, is the reference for every plotted percentage.

The question asks: “¿Cuál es, a su juicio, el principal problema que existe actualmente en España? ¿Y el segundo? ¿Y el tercero?” Up to three spontaneously named national problems are coded; the total is the percentage of all respondents naming each topic among their answers, not the share of all mentions, the first response alone, or a vote for/against a policy. People can name several issues; percentages must not be interpreted as mutually exclusive. The sampling population is Spanish citizens aged at least 18; use of phone rather than in-person interviews since 2023 and question position effects limit long comparisons. September observations are snapshots, not annual means.

The 2023 and 2024 category labels for these three issues match, and both surveys were conducted by telephone. Immigration rose 24.7 percentage points, climate fell 10.1 points, and gender violence fell 0.8 points across these two September snapshots. These show a change in named priorities, not a measured shift in respondents' immigration or climate attitudes. The question occupied a different position in each questionnaire (question 4 in 2023 and 9 in 2024), and their field dates and sample sizes differ. No causal explanation or formal significance test follows directly from the marginals. Since 2025, the PDF identifies this question as recoded; check the coding protocols before treating changes as exact like-for-like effects.

The gender-violence code reads `La violencia contra la mujer` in 2018 and `La violencia de género` in 2019 and later. The 1.9% to 6.6% difference is therefore sensitive to a coding-label change. Ask for coding manuals and, where available, original verbatim responses before interpreting the magnitude. A category labelled environmental problems appears in earlier studies, but neither its label nor its relation to the later, separate climate category permits treating it as an equivalent substitute. Missing early separate climate values are missing, not zeros.

Gender-based violence and racism are narrow topics, not a comprehensive measure of gender identity, LGBTQ rights, discrimination, or cultural issues. Immigration is a national problem in this question, but a response does not reveal whether the respondent prioritizes integration, restriction or protection of migrants. The unpublished design-specific variances and weighted percentages cannot be reconstructed from aggregate tables alone. In-person quota sampling and weighted telephone samples also make naïve binomial confidence intervals unjustified. Microdata, weights, design information and coding protocols are required for valid uncertainty analyses.

Two independent published analyses of the 2018 Brazilian election give useful competing accounts. Rennó's 2018 election panel (March, September and October) finds a relationship between support for Bolsonaro and cultural issue positions alongside party opposition, law and order and economic liberalism ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/lap.2020.13)). Setzler uses 1,498 face-to-face AmericasBarometer interviews, mainly in February 2019, including 1,006 reported voters, and finds that measures of sexism and homophobia are not statistically significant predictors after controls. His model gives a 56% versus 53% probability contrast for the typical controversial-attitudes profile of Bolsonaro versus other voters, and 64% versus 43% for the corresponding antipetismo contrast ([article](https://new.scielo.br/j/bpsr/a/YWW55BRxPQTZKqfcyhw3f6L/?ilang=en)). The datasets, questions, sampling and models differ. Neither estimate is a causal effect or an issue-salience measure, and both are authors' calculations, not an independent reanalysis. Together they show why campaign rhetoric, correlations with attitudes and motives for vote choice need separate evidence.
