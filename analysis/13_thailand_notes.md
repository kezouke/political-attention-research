# Thailand: a short comparable series, campaign documents and election results

This is an evidence note, not a continuous 2010–2026 time series. Script `python analysis/13_thailand_longitudinal.py` transcribes the published figures and recreates the three `data/processed/thailand_*.csv` tables. The values are rounded in the source, so derived changes are descriptive. A public copy of the original 2010 survey report, retained locally as `data/raw/thailand_asia_foundation_2010.pdf`, has SHA-256 `4c1ebbc120d0d0bc2818b7618b64767e7e32845f0c1892e3582d78851b692c12`. Raw reports may not be redistributable and need not be committed.

## Political conflict before and after the 2010 crisis

The [Asia Foundation survey report](https://www.locjkt.or.id/application/docliblcjkt/upload/ThailandNationalSurvey2010.pdf), printed p. 25, compares answers to “In your view, what is the biggest problem facing Thailand?” Political conflict rose from **24% in 2009 to 42% in 2010**, a change of **18 percentage points**. If the two percentages were rounded to the nearest integer, the underlying difference is approximately 17–19 points. This interval reflects rounding only, not sampling uncertainty.

The [2009 Asia Foundation press release](https://www.newmandala.org/wp-content/uploads/2009/09/Thai_Constitution_Conference_Press_Release_ENG-1.pdf) confirms the 2009 percentages and fieldwork dates. The 2009 interviews ran June 13–July 5; the 2010 interviews September 17–October 23. Each comparison uses 1,500 respondents from the five original geographic strata. The 2010 survey added 100 observations in Pattani, Yala and Narathiwat. Those areas are excluded from the published comparisons, because they were absent in 2009 (methodology, pp. 173–175). The clustered stratified design retains previously selected provinces; a simple independent random sample confidence interval would be misleading. With no respondent-level data and design variances, there is no defensible design-based significance test here. The May 2010 conflict and deaths in Bangkok provide a plausible historical context, not a causal estimate. The survey predates the national 2011 election and does not quantify campaign messages.

**Important transcription conflict:** the same report states the 2010 “poor economy” share as 24% in the p. 25 graph, 33% in the adjacent paragraph and 35% in the executive summary (p. 9). All three cannot be right for the same measure. The dataset records that value as missing. The 2009 poor-economy share of 60% appears consistently, but no 2009–2010 change is computed. The political-conflict figures agree between the chart and prose.

## Later survey snapshots do not make a trend

The [Thammasat University and Marketbuzzz 2023 release](https://sgs.tu.ac.th/news/top-public-concern-in-thailand-2023/) reports 42% for environment among 1,000 respondents surveyed in April 2023. Its [2021 release](https://sgs.tu.ac.th/news/the-mas-program-the-school-of-global-studies-thammasat-university-and-marketbuzz-collaborated-on-the-survey-topic-are-the-majority-of-thai-people-concerned-about-the-environment/) reports 38% among 1,000 respondents. These reports do not provide the original questionnaire, sampling frame, recruitment mode, exact fieldwork days or usable design details. The releases describe a list of top concerns; they do not establish a fixed one-choice climate question. Environmental concern could include local air pollution. We do not compute a 2021–2023 change or infer declining climate salience.

[Gallup World Poll](https://news.gallup.com/poll/701897/economy-dominates-issues-ahead-thai-election.aspx) in September–November 2025 asked an open-ended question about the most important problem facing the country. Gallup reports economy at **48%** and safety and security at **13%**. This was just before the February 2026 election, but not a question about voting intention. The release does not give the Thailand-specific sample size, and the item, categories and sampling differ from Asia Foundation and Thammasat. There is no valid numerical trend between these snapshots. Neither a 2010–2026 change in Thai climate concern nor a change in the priority of gender identity can be estimated from these sources.

## Campaigns: proposals in the same party are not mutually exclusive

Move Forward's [March 17, 2023 consultation document](https://www.moveforwardparty.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/%E0%B8%AB%E0%B8%99%E0%B8%B1%E0%B8%87%E0%B8%AA%E0%B8%B7%E0%B8%AD-%E0%B8%81%E0%B8%81.%E0%B8%81%E0%B8%AD%E0%B8%A5-2-66-%E0%B8%82%E0%B8%AD%E0%B8%A3%E0%B8%B1%E0%B8%9A%E0%B8%9F%E0%B8%B1%E0%B8%87%E0%B8%84%E0%B8%A7%E0%B8%B2%E0%B8%A1%E0%B8%84%E0%B8%B4%E0%B8%94%E0%B9%80%E0%B8%AB%E0%B9%87%E0%B8%99%E0%B9%80%E0%B8%81%E0%B8%B5%E0%B9%88%E0%B8%A2%E0%B8%A7%E0%B8%81%E0%B8%B1%E0%B8%9A%E0%B8%99%E0%B9%82%E0%B8%A2%E0%B8%9A%E0%B8%B2%E0%B8%A2.pdf) asks local party representatives to comment on proposed election policies and includes a new constitution, economic and social provisions, and marriage equality. Its [election policy website](https://election66.moveforwardparty.org/policy/collection/PolicyCategory/5/10) also proposes a 450 baht daily minimum wage. Rival Pheu Thai's [April 12, 2023 campaign statement](https://www.ptp.or.th/archives/26848) proposes a 10,000 baht digital wallet. These primary documents establish the presence of several issue types, including an identity-related proposal, in the campaign period. They do not establish the percentage of campaign attention, comparisons between election years, public preferences, or why people voted.

The institutional context matters. Despite Move Forward winning the largest number of seats in 2023, selection of the prime minister also required participation by the appointed Senate. The constitutional court dissolved Move Forward in 2024 in connection with its campaign for reform of the royal-defamation law. The [UK House of Commons research briefing](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10141/CBP-10141.pdf) documents the institutional process; it does not show why individual voters chose a party. The People's Party is its political successor, not the same legal entity.

## Electoral arithmetic, not a measure of issue salience

The successor comparison is a descriptive difference of approximately −6.96 points, not a vote migration estimate. In 2026 Bhumjaithai won 18.46% of party-choice votes but 38.4% of the 500 seats. One reason is that 400 seats were decided in separate constituency races; list votes do not determine all seats. The March 5 ECT report records 191 Bhumjaithai seats among 499 certified seats; the IPU's final outcome reports 192 of 500. These are different certification snapshots, not competing results for the same date.

## Remaining gap

No independently validated, comparable Thai-language campaign or media corpus was obtained for multiple election cycles. These survey and documentary additions do not establish a country-wide long-run ranking of economics, climate and identity. Original instruments, microdata with design variables, dated comparable party texts and a manually checked Thai-language coding sample would be needed before making that claim.
