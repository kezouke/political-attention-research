# Brazil: what people named as the main national problem

## Question and sample

Ten independent Latinobarómetro waves (2010, 2011, 2013, 2015–2018, 2020, 2023 and 2024) ask an open national most-important-problem question with one answer. The 2010 English questionnaire asks, “In your opinion, which is the most important problem in the country?” and instructs interviewers to write the answer as given and accept one answer. The [2024 questionnaire](https://www.latinobarometro.org/documents/LAT-2024/latinobarometro-2024-questionnaire-eng.pdf) uses materially similar wording. Earlier [2010 questionnaire](https://www.latinobarometro.org/documents/LAT-2010/latinobarometro-2010-eng.pdf). Each wave is a separate sample; this is not a panel of the same respondents. In Brazil the target population is aged 16 and older, with n=1,204 in both endpoint waves. The 2024 Brazil fieldwork ran from 31 August to 18 September using face-to-face interviews, four sampling stages and quotas in the last stage ([technical record](https://www.latinobarometro.org/documents/LAT-2024/latinobarometro-2024-technical-records.pdf)). The [2010 technical report](https://www.latinobarometro.org/documents/LAT-2010/informemetodologico-latinobarometro-2010.pdf) describes three probability stages followed by quotas. Its table of contents contains an internal 2015 heading despite the body identifying the 2010 wave. Sampling and mode can change in intervening years. Fieldwork months are read from microdata, not imputed.

Primary values divide by all respondents, including don't know, refusal and no problem. The sensitivity column divides by respondents who give one of the documented substantive/other/no-problem codes. We use the source `wt` weight: in the Brazilian sample it equals 1 in all ten waves. Sample counts and rates are estimates for the survey's sampling frame, not measures of personal priorities for all issues.

## Coding and uncertainty

Responses are pre-coded by the original survey organisation, not by us. We group these categories with the wave-specific crosswalk in `data/processed/brazil_mip_code_crosswalk.csv`; code numbers and meanings changed in 2018 and 2023. Economy-strict includes low wages, unstable employment, inflation/cost, unemployment and the general economy as the wave's existing categories permit. Economy-broad adds poverty, income inequality or social injustice. The 2010–2011 code combining instability in employment and immigration is excluded from both main economy estimates and recorded separately (`economy_ambiguous`) as a sensitivity check. In 2010, the inflation/price category also contains 'economic crisis', unlike later waves. The environment-strict category combines environment and global warming; a separately coded pollution category appears from 2018 and is included only in environment-expanded. The racial discrimination code measures one specific concern and cannot represent all identity or LGBT issues. Categories are based on spontaneous first choices; a small percentage does not imply the topic is unimportant, unknown or unsupported.

The provided 95% Wilson intervals use the Kish weight-only effective sample size and an independent-binomial approximation. Endpoint differences use an independent-wave normal approximation. They are descriptive sampling-sensitivity intervals, **not design-based confidence intervals**: the calculations do not account for quotas, clustering, stratification, frame coverage, measurement or coding error. They should not be used to assert statistical significance or its absence. The alternative denominator and broader category definitions are included to expose sensitivity rather than select a preferred result.

In the primary denominator, strict economic mentions are 17.19% in 2010 and 17.11% in 2024 (difference −0.08 percentage points; independent-binomial approximation −3.09 to +2.93 pp). Among substantive/other/no-problem answers the corresponding difference is +1.20 pp. Broad economic mentions are 20.68% and 22.18% (+1.50 pp; IID approximation −1.78 to +4.77 pp). The ambiguous 2010 employment/immigration category accounts for 0.17% of the full sample. Corruption was named by 2.91% in 2010, 31.17% in 2017 and 4.40% in 2024. This irregular series does not support a simple secular increase in the selected top issue. Environment/global warming was named by 8/1,204 in 2010 and 14/1,204 in 2024. These low counts and the one-answer format do not rule out broader concern about climate. Health was named by 28.16% and 21.43% respectively, an important counterexample to treating the agenda as a choice between only climate and the economy.

## Reproduce

Download the official Stata archives for each year from `https://www.latinobarometro.org/latinobarometro-YYYY` (the 2020 wave has fieldwork in 2020), name them `data/raw/lat_YYYY.zip`, and run `python analysis/08_brazil_and_six_country_opinion.py`. The file accepts the English `.dta` member in each official archive, and exports checksums of the exact zip snapshots in `data/processed/brazil_mip_provenance.csv`. The provider's [terms](https://www.latinobarometro.org/study-level) permit non-commercial research and prohibit redistribution of the raw datasets; zip files remain excluded from Git. The outputs contain only aggregate results and the category crosswalk. Source documents and results should be cited as Latinobarómetro Study YYYY, Latinobarómetro Corporation, wave YYYY, aggregated version, `https://www.latinobarometro.org/latinobarometro-YYYY`.

## Additional country coverage and non-comparability

The same script exports existing SPES/Eurobarometer observations for Spain, Bulgaria and Serbia, including years previously omitted from the public country's table. In 2010–2024 unemployment mentions fell 72.15% to 20.58% in Spain and 53.41% to 8.86% in Bulgaria, while price mentions rose 9.84% to 37.90% and 21.80% to 47.81%. Serbia’s available comparison begins 2012, not 2010: unemployment 47.84% to 9.62%, prices 26.57% to 47.58%. SPES harmonises Eurobarometer issue responses and permits up to two choices; year 2011 is missing from this snapshot. The environment category combines environment, climate and energy security, so its movement cannot be interpreted as climate alone. No respondent-level uncertainties are supplied with this export. [SPES methods](https://www.sustainabilityperformances.eu/spes-explorer/).

Neither the top-one open Latinobarómetro figures nor these prompted top-two SPES figures can be pooled or directly compared as if they used the same instrument. Identity-related salience is unmeasured in the SPES categories used here. The data cannot resolve whether climate or identity dominated politics in 2010 or whether a social-media feed distorted its author's impression.

## Cross-check against the provider's published table

The 2024 [results-by-country PDF](https://www.latinobarometro.org/documents/LAT-2024/latinobarometro-2024-results-by-country.pdf), printed page 37, reports **column percentages**, not the percentage of Brazilian respondents within each problem category. Its health-problem column has 766 weighted responses across countries and reports Brazil's share of that column as 33.7%; multiplying these gives approximately 258, consistent with the 258 Brazilian microdata cases. Dividing 258 by the 1,204 Brazilian respondents gives 21.43%. The apparent 33.7% in that PDF is therefore not Brazil's prevalence and must not be used as one. The PDF's regional weighted totals are not the same as sums of unweighted microdata cases.
