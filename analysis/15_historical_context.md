# Historical context, political mechanisms and competing explanations

This note supplies evidence for interpreting the descriptive series. It does not identify the causal effect of a crisis, legal investigation, media campaign or party strategy on any survey response. It separates source facts, authors' statistical findings and hypotheses for this project. Dates use the period of the event or measurement, not the date of the publication describing it.

## An explanatory framework

**Working hypothesis: shocks change which problems are urgent, while institutions, party strategies and media determine how the shock is named and acted upon.** A financial crisis can raise fear of job loss; a price shock can reduce the buying power of those still employed; a corruption investigation can make government integrity salient; and a war can make security salient. These pressures coexist with climate concern and with cultural and group conflicts. A survey that allows only one or two responses measures the relative ranking at that moment, not the disappearance of every unchosen concern. This is a testable framework rather than a demonstrated universal law.

The framework has three distinct steps. (1) **Exposure:** a household faces a price increase, job loss or other shock; exposure differs by occupation, assets, region and welfare protection. (2) **Interpretation:** people identify a problem and may attribute responsibility to institutions, politicians, outsiders or structural conditions. Personal experience, news and organised political actors can supply different interpretations. (3) **Political mobilisation:** parties choose which issues and positions to emphasise, while journalists and platforms select content; electoral rules and institutional constraints determine which issues enter the contest and how they translate into government. Observing a price shock and a change in a survey ranking establishes timing, not those three links or their direction. A change in an issue's salience is not necessarily a change in an individual's ideological position.

The [Comparative Policy Agendas study of the public agenda](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198835332.003.0025) distinguishes changes in real-world problems from the meaning assigned to those facts, and evaluates public, media and policy attention across countries. A recent [review of salience formation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/italian-political-science-review-rivista-italiana-di-scienza-politica/article/sound-of-salience-a-tridimensional-perspective-on-issue-salience-formation/FB70D360F9B5BAEC6A23E35FDB533B9F) describes public, partisan and media salience as separate and potentially reciprocal. That review is a conceptual synthesis; it does not establish that a specific medium caused the priorities in our data.

Political parties have incentives to highlight issues on which they are seen as competent or can mobilise a favourable coalition. An opposing party can instead try to shift the contest to another issue, and a party may soften its position on a divisive subject while speaking less about it. See the studies of [strategic diffusion and issue competition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/learning-at-home-and-abroad-how-competition-conditions-the-diffusion-of-party-strategies/214E8DDA6FF7ABB153E0A8E522926EE4) and [silent responsiveness to wedge issues](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-political-research/article/silent-responsiveness-how-public-opinion-affects-party-discourse-on-wedge-issues/0076FBA2C0882BBD9FA600D813907439). These mechanisms predict differences across parties in the same election, not merely a national agenda that all candidates reproduce.

**Alternative explanations to keep live:** changing question options or survey mode; preference change rather than only reordering; different party supply or credible challengers; differential media attention; changes in population composition; and common shocks across countries. Climate attitudes may also be sensitive to the economic cycle. [Kenny (2020)](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/80667/), using World Values Survey data before and after the Great Recession in advanced democracies and Latin America, reports a negative association between national unemployment and choosing the environment over economic growth. Growth/GDP did not show the same association, and recession did not alter the relationship between individual wealth and environmental prioritisation in the author's analysis. The forced trade-off question differs from our ESS climate-worry question. This evidence challenges a claim that economic context only changes rankings and never changes environmental preferences; neither analysis proves a universal trade-off.

For immigration, [Hainmueller and Hopkins (2014)](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-polisci-102512-194818) review research and find limited accumulated support for explanations based primarily on personal labour-market self-interest. They highlight perceptions of nationwide cultural consequences and, to a lesser extent, perceived national economic effects, while stressing the need for stronger causal identification. This is research about **attitudes towards immigration**, not a measurement of how prominent immigration is relative to prices in a given election. Economic and cultural interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and identity politics is broader than immigration.

## 2008–2014: Spain's financial crisis and representation

Spain's construction and property boom was financed by credit, creating concentrated bank exposure to real estate. When the bubble burst in 2008/09, loan losses and a recession undermined the financial sector. In 2012–13, the European Stability Mechanism disbursed €41.33 billion of bank-recapitalisation loans to Spain, out of a possible €100 billion. Its programme imposed bank-sector conditions; it should not be described as €100 billion actually disbursed or as identical to a full sovereign adjustment programme. [European Stability Mechanism](https://www.esm.europa.eu/assistance/spain).

Spain's official national labour-force survey puts average annual unemployment at **26.09% in 2013** and **11.34% in 2024**. These rates use unemployed persons divided by the labour force, not the share of survey respondents citing unemployment as one of their top two issues. [INE series EPA648630](https://ine.es/consul/serie.do?d=true&s=EPA648630). The project's harmonised Eurobarometer series independently reports unemployment mentions of 72.1% in 2010 and 20.6% in 2024. Their broad co-movement is consistent with changing exposure to unemployment but does not measure an individual-level causal effect; the 2011 polling gap, age composition and survey response options also matter.

The political sequence was not automatic. Social mobilisation included the 15-M/Indignados movement in 2011; the incumbent PSOE lost the 2011 election, and the incoming PP pursued austerity while the banking system was restructured. Podemos formed in 2014 and obtained **7.98% of Spain's vote and five seats in the May 2014 European Parliament election**. [European Parliament official election summary](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/presse/eurobarometres/2018/625195/PE-EB_SA%282018%29625195_XL.pdf). The simultaneous experience of mass unemployment, cuts and banking support offered openings for an accountability/representation frame; the numerical associations do not establish which event caused which vote.

The peer-reviewed literature supports a more differentiated mechanism than “economic losers voted for Podemos.” [Ramiro and Gómez (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0032321716647400) analyse five cross-sectional Spanish surveys from May 2014 to April 2015 and find that Podemos support relative to the already established radical left is linked to protest, anti-mainstream sentiment and unmet expectations, rather than fitting a simple socioeconomic-loser profile. [Bosch and Durán (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1354068817710223), using a multinomial model of preferences for Podemos and Ciudadanos, report an association both with negative national economic assessments and perceptions of corruption, including their interaction. These are observational findings about preferences, not direct causal estimates from our own survey series.

## 2014–2018: Brazil, recession, corruption and campaign strategy

Brazil's recession and political crisis overlapped. A World Bank account reports real GDP growth of **−3.5% in 2015 and −3.3% in 2016**, with external conditions, domestic fiscal imbalances, structural bottlenecks and uncertainty all cited as contributing context. This publication's historical values should not be silently substituted for current revised GDP releases. [World Bank, 2019](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/210521559008938530/pdf/Brazil-Mato-Grosso-Fiscal-Adjustment-and-Environmental-Sustanability-Development-Policy-Loan.pdf).

The Lava Jato investigation began in 2014. It revealed corruption involving Petrobras contracts and implicated politicians across parties as well as business figures. The investigation, leaks and press coverage made specific allegations publicly visible. Dilma Rousseff's 2016 impeachment occurred in that political environment, but its **formal Senate judgment** concerned budget-related responsibility offences: credit operations with a state-controlled financial institution and budget decrees without congressional authorisation. The Senate voted 61–20 on 31 August 2016. Her removal is not proof she was convicted of Petrobras bribery. [Federal Senate judgment](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/arquivos/2016/08/31/veja-a-sentenca-de-impeachment-contra-dilma-rousseff). The legal and political legitimacy of impeachment was contested.

In the project's Latinobarómetro microdata, corruption was the unprompted single top national problem for **2.9% in 2010, 31.2% in 2017 and 4.4% in 2024**. These are different respondent samples and the first choices, not a measure of the actual level of corruption; see `analysis/08_opinion_notes.md` for coding, quota sampling and denominator. The 2014–2017 sequence makes scandal exposure and elite communication plausible explanations for heightened attention, but this correlation does not separately identify their effects from the economic recession, political conflict and changing trust.

Campaigns show an additional mechanism. [Lopes, Albuquerque and Bezerra (2020)](https://doi.org/10.15448/1984-7289.2020.3.37248) examine **distinct free TV campaign productions** in the 12–26 October 2018 presidential second round, not a random sample of all media and not the number of broadcasts. Their coding reports corruption in **10 of 11 Bolsonaro programmes versus 2 of 14 Haddad programmes**. The article's 57.2% estimate refers to mentions in its own selected lexical/discourse categories, not percentage of all campaign time, programme text or voters. This is a concrete example of competing parties allocating attention differently to the same public problem. The authors interpret the anti-PT and anti-system frame as closely linked to the investigation; this interpretation is not a causal estimate.

Bolsonaro received **55.13% of valid votes** against Haddad's 44.87% in the official final second-round result. [Brazilian electoral court](https://www.tse.jus.br/comunicacao/noticias/2018/Outubro/eleicoes-2018-justica-eleitoral-conclui-totalizacao-dos-votos-do-segundo-turno). [Rennó (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1017/lap.2020.13), analysing the 2018 Brazilian Electoral Panel Study, reports that anti-PT feeling, cultural backlash, law-and-order views and economic liberalism were all associated with support for Bolsonaro. Issue positions and vote choice are different measurements from frequency of mentions. The study challenges a corruption-only explanation of that election without proving how much any one issue caused the result.

## 2020–2025: the inflation and purchasing-power shock

The euro-area price shock did not begin with the invasion alone. The ECB's [2024 decomposition](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpops/ecb.op343~ab3e870d21.en.pdf) models inflation after pandemic closures as reflecting reopening demand, supply constraints, energy and food shocks, and policy conditions. In its model, energy price shocks contributed strongly from Q2 2021 through Q1 2023, with Russia's 2022 invasion and related supply disruption adding to the pressure. Estimated model contributions depend on the model and identification assumptions and are not direct experimental evidence.

In **October 2022** the euro-area annual HICP inflation rate was **10.6%**; Eurostat's contribution accounting attributes **4.44 percentage points** of that rate to energy. Spain's HICP was 7.3% and Bulgaria's 14.8%. [Eurostat release, 17 November 2022](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/en/web/products-euro-indicators/-/2-17112022-ap). Contributions are weighted accounting decompositions, not the causal effects of the war or any individual policy.

Purchasing power can remain impaired after headline inflation falls: a fall in the inflation rate means prices rise more slowly, not that the price level has returned to its previous level. The OECD reports that Spain's **real hourly wages in Q1 2025 remained 4.2% below Q1 2021**, even after nominal wage increases in 2023–24. This is an average wage measure, not a measure of the income of every household. As a contrasting distributional case, the OECD estimates Spain's statutory minimum wage in April 2025 was 3.1% higher in real terms than in January 2021. [OECD Employment Outlook 2025: Spain](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2025-country-notes_f91531f7-en/spain_ded48eec-en.html).

Together, declining unemployment and a subsequent loss of real purchasing power make the project's shift from unemployment to prices as top national problems substantively plausible. They do not show that an individual respondent personally lost purchasing power or that wages alone caused their answer. Policy interventions, energy dependence, food shares, housing costs and survey timing can produce different patterns across countries despite common shocks. The project's weaker inflation coefficient after adding year indicators warns that common shocks make country-specific causal attribution difficult.

## What the data would need to discriminate between explanations

- Repeated **same-wording** rankings and independent ratings of climate importance, with microdata for the same country and period, would distinguish a changing rank from a change in underlying concern. ESS8 and ESS10 show strong worry among valid respondents, but their worry question is not the same as Eurobarometer's top-two question and their fieldwork and modes differ.
- Survey panels or repeated individual observations linked to local shocks could examine whether a person's change in economic exposure precedes a change in concern, subject to time-varying confounding and attrition. Aggregate country-level co-movement cannot make that inference.
- A comparative campaign study needs candidate- and period-specific text, a common unit, documented selection and coding validation. Positive or negative positions, issue attention and campaign audience exposure are separate quantities.
- The user's six countries differ in exposure, institutional setting and measurement coverage. No common mechanism should be asserted for all six merely because some case histories fit a common shock narrative.

## Additional research review: economic shocks, information, resources and climate policy

### Economic crisis, trust and anti-establishment voting

Yann Algan, Sergei Guriev, Elias Papaioannou and Evgenia Passari, [“The European Trust Crisis and the Rise of Populism” (2017)](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/algantextfa17bpea.pdf), compare 220 European NUTS-2 regions across 26 countries around the Great Recession. Their panel estimates associate a one percentage point increase in unemployment with about a one percentage point increase in the populist vote share. They also find that greater unemployment and reduced trust in national and EU political institutions move together, whereas the relationship with interpersonal trust is weaker. The association with attitudes towards immigrants is muted, particularly for perceived cultural impact. The political outcome is the party vote share, not the fraction of survey respondents naming the economy as their top problem.

The authors also use the pre-crisis construction share of regional value added as an instrument for unemployment changes. Their two-stage estimates associate one percentage point higher instrument-predicted unemployment with 2 to 4.4 percentage points more anti-establishment votes, depending on specification (paper p. 345). These are not interchangeable with the approximately one-point panel association: the outcome category and estimation design differ. The instrument is plausible because construction exposure predicted vulnerability to the housing crash, but its exclusion restriction remains contestable: a construction bust can affect voting via household wealth, debts, evictions or local fiscal strain as well as measured unemployment. The authors explicitly recognize that pre-crisis specialization is not fully exogenous. The paper does not identify a causal mediation sequence in which unemployment first reduces trust and reduced trust then causes party choice. Trust and votes could also respond separately to a shared shock. Spain and Bulgaria are part of the European scope, but a pooled coefficient is not a country-specific estimate; it should not be transferred to Serbia, Brazil, Israel or Thailand.

The result suggests a testable mechanism for our salience study: material shocks may raise demands for competence and institutional change, and parties with different ideologies may channel the same grievance. It does not establish that economic shocks alone displaced cultural concerns. Reverse causality, differential exposure, party supply and concurrent institutional crises remain credible competing explanations.

### Information, government confidence and political competition

Sergei Guriev and Daniel Treisman, [“Informational Autocrats” (2019)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.33.4.100), and their formal [“A Theory of Informational Autocracy” (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2020.104158), propose that rulers with imperfectly observed competence can sustain support through propaganda, censorship and co-opting informed elites. The 2019 paper documents historical patterns consistent with this model; the 2020 paper models competing equilibria. Neither proves that every authoritarian regime relies primarily on information control, that coercion disappears, or that an observer's particular social-media feed reflects a national agenda. Political regimes and available technologies change over time.

A separate empirical paper by Sergei Guriev, Nikita Melnikov and Ekaterina Zhuravskaya, [“3G Internet and Confidence in Government” (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjaa040), analyzes Gallup World Poll responses from 840,537 people in 2,232 regions in 116 countries during 2008–2017. The authors compare expansion of mobile broadband with government approval and use regional lightning frequency, which raises the costs of building and maintaining mobile infrastructure, as an instrument for rollout. Their identification needs lightning to affect changes in approval through 3G rather than other regional differences or shocks, conditional on the model's controls. They report lower confidence as 3G expands where internet access is uncensored, with larger effects where traditional media are censored. In their European electoral sample they also report lower incumbent and higher populist opposition vote shares. Their [author briefing](https://freepolicybriefs.org/2021/02/08/implications-mobile-internet/) compares the estimated decade-long expansion effect on approval with that of a 2.2 percentage point rise in national unemployment; these are model-based comparisons, not 2.2 percentage points of approval.

The 3G paper is about connectivity and confidence, not direct measurement of climate, identity or the selection algorithm of an individual feed. The European electoral finding does not automatically describe elections in the six countries. The informational-autocracy and 3G papers are compatible with different outcomes: online access can expose corruption, but its effects depend on censorship, information supply and politics. Greater connectivity can also expose people to misinformation or selective content; this study does not isolate the contribution of recommendation algorithms.

### Resource dependence and state capacity

Andrey Movchan, Alexander Zotin and Vladimir Grigoryev, [“Managing the Resource Curse: Strategies of Oil-Dependent Economies in the Modern Era” (2017)](https://carnegieendowment.org/files/200317_CarnegieReport_2017_layouts.pdf), is a Carnegie comparative policy report, not a peer-reviewed causal identification study. Its ten cases include Venezuela, Angola, Mexico, Azerbaijan, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iran, Nigeria and Norway. The authors discuss ways resource revenues, commodity volatility, exchange rates, political institutions and diversification interact, with policy lessons intended particularly for Russia. Brazil and the other five named cases are not part of this ten-country comparison. The report cannot by itself establish how much resource rents caused corruption, rising prices or a change in electoral salience in these six countries.

The comparison is useful because fiscal dependence can change the resources available for redistribution, incentives for rent-seeking and exposure to external shocks. But outcomes are conditional, not predetermined: Frederick van der Ploeg's peer-reviewed survey [“Natural Resources: Curse or Blessing?” (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.49.2.366) reviews evidence that either outcome is possible, depending among other things on volatility, institutions, financial development and investment. Resource rents are a hypothesis about political and fiscal constraints, not evidence that any given country follows a uniform resource-curse path.

### Climate policy, household costs and political trust: France as a comparison

Thomas Douenne and Adrien Fabre, [“Yellow Vests, Pessimistic Beliefs, and Carbon Tax Aversion” (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20200092), investigate a hypothetical carbon tax whose proceeds are redistributed equally to adults. Their quota-based online survey of 3,002 French respondents was fielded in February–March 2019 after the Yellow Vests protests; it is not a representative sample of protesters. They compare respondents' perceptions of effects with household expenditure-based estimates and randomly vary information. Respondents substantially overestimate their losses and underestimate the policy's environmental effectiveness. Information treatments shift beliefs, but only modestly. In the authors' [policy account](https://greenfiscalpolicy.org/blog/the-distributional-effects-of-carbon-taxation-lessons-from-the-french-yellow-vests-movement/), the modelled policy would financially benefit about 70% of households, yet only about 10% support it. These numbers refer to this specific hypothetical full-dividend design and modelling assumptions, not the actual 2018 French fuel-tax policy.

This example identifies a plausible interaction between environmental policy and economic pressure. Support depends not only on attitudes to climate but also on expected costs, distribution, feasibility of substitution and trust in implementation. The result does not imply that concern for climate fell, that every opponent was misinformed, or that a dividend erases differences between high- and low-fuel-use households at the same income. France provides a mechanism to investigate, not a measured effect for Spain, Serbia, Brazil, Israel, Thailand or Bulgaria. The paper's [replication package](https://github.com/thomasdouenne/yellow_vests_aej_ep) documents the expenditure data and model used for the distributional calculation.
