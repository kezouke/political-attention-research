# Crisis and electoral gains

This note documents the comparison in [When does a crisis strengthen populists?](docs/index.html). It separates the main election comparison, historical mechanisms and the later price episode. The previous essay on public priorities remains in [the archive](docs/priorities.html).

## What is measured

The outcome is the change, in percentage points, in the sum of the valid-vote shares of campaigns classified as populist. We use the [World Populism Dataset (WPD) methodology](https://world-populism-dataset.github.io/website/data.html) and its public dashboard's versioned data file. The authors look for campaigns that portray ordinary people as excluded from political power by a self-serving elite. They generally code campaigns from 5% of the vote upwards. A zero is a coded election with no qualifying campaign in this release. It can include a small campaign or a campaign that the authors did not classify as populist. A gain can reflect more votes for an existing party, a new candidacy or a changed campaign classification.

The study keeps lower-house or unicameral parliamentary elections separate from first-round direct elections for the national executive. For parallel ballots with both list and constituency values in WPD, it uses the list figure. The WPD percentage is retained where the provider supplies an aggregate for an alliance. PPEG shares used to track the governing party may weight multiple tiers. Seat shares, votes, and the ability to form a government answer different questions.

## Election calendar and crisis dates

The source calendar covers 50 countries. We first retain first-round elections. In countries also covered by [PPEG 2025v1](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/5OAH7P), a WPD date is matched to the corresponding PPEG election type. An exact date or a unique PPEG date within 14 days is used. Any PPEG-only election from the first observed WPD election in a country and type through December 2020 enters the calendar with a missing vote classification. This prevents the comparison from skipping a known intervening ballot and treating the next classified one as the first election. WPD-only dates remain where the cross-check did not match. The calendar is a working research record, and not every date or classification has been independently verified. For example, the source represents Argentina's staggered 2003 legislative election by a single date. The 2003 comparison should be read at the election-cycle level.

We excluded a duplicate row for the Bloc Québécois that the dashboard assigned to 2011 even though its original date field says 2019. The retained 2011 value is 6.10%, close to the 6.05% reported by PPEG. We kept Brazil's official first-round comparison and excluded all runoffs. The WPD dashboard file gives Bolsonaro 51.13% in the second round, whereas the [electoral court reports 55.13%](https://www.tse.jus.br/comunicacao/noticias/2018/Outubro/eleicoes-2018-justica-eleitoral-conclui-totalizacao-dos-votos-do-segundo-turno). Its 46.03% for the [first round](https://www.tse.jus.br/comunicacao/noticias/2018/Outubro/concluida-totalizacao-de-votos-do-1o-turno-das-eleicoes-2018) agrees with the official result.

Banking episodes use [Laeven and Valencia (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41308-020-00107-3), including the workbook's onset month where given. The published article and workbook cover the entire 1990–2015 onset window. Borderline cases flagged by the authors are recorded and excluded from the main totals. Other recessions start at the first negative-growth calendar year following a nonnegative year in [World Development Indicators real GDP growth](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG). To separate these from banking-linked contractions, exclude starts with a banking crisis overlapping the year before, the same year or the year after. This is a transparent annual rule for the first observed year of real contraction. It is distinct from quarterly business-cycle dating and from income losses for particular households.

An event has an earliest and latest onset date. These are the first and last day of the month for month-dated crises, or January 1 and December 31 for year-dated crises. The pre-election is the last eligible election before the earliest date, within six years. The post-election is the first eligible election after the latest date, within five years in the main comparison or three in the short window. If an election falls inside the uncertain onset interval, the main comparison leaves that pair out. The broader timing check retains it and still uses the elections outside the interval. Main and broader timing samples require that both election years are classified by [V-Dem Regimes of the World](https://v-dem.net/data/dataset-archive/) as electoral or liberal democracies. The regime check is country-year data, which can miss political changes within a year. A sensitivity table also reports the results without the regime filter.

The resulting candidate table retains each country, onset, election type and window, including missing classifications or a missing election in the window after the start of the country's source calendar. The main five-year sample contains 62 parliamentary pairs and 16 first-round presidential pairs in 37 countries in total. Some countries and elections appear in more than one episode. These are descriptive episode comparisons. A simultaneous global shock can affect many observations, and these comparisons do not supply independent causal estimates.

## Benchmarks and checks

The benchmark uses consecutive election pairs from countries that contribute crisis pairs for that election type. Each intervening calendar year, including the two election years, must have observed nonnegative real GDP growth and no systemic banking crisis in progress. This benchmark shows how often and how far electoral support also shifted in calmer periods. It does not estimate what would have happened without each specific crisis. Countries can contribute different numbers of benchmark pairs.

The governing-party comparison follows the party of the head of government in PPEG's latest recorded cabinet before the post-crisis election. It compares that same party's shares at both elections. An unresolved party, missing value or ambiguous vote share excludes the pair from this calculation. Changes in this party's and other parties' aggregate shares do not identify individual vote switching. Turnout is available in the pair table when PPEG provides both total ballots and the electorate. We use it as context and do not infer individual mobilisation from it.

The [main results and sensitivities](data/processed/crisis_election_summary.csv) show the direction, median, mean and quartiles of each comparison. The three-year window uses fewer pairs. Excluding 2007–2009 starts checks dependence on the global financial crisis. The range of changes is more informative than a single coefficient. For other recessions, allowing an intervening vote in the uncertain onset year changes the median from 4.51 to 0.31 points in the five-year comparison. This result should not be used to rank crisis types by their effects.

[V-Party v2](https://doi.org/10.23696/vpartydsv2) supplies an independent measure for a subset of the same parliamentary elections. Its expert-coded populism index combines anti-elitism and people-centrism on a 0–1 scale. For an election with scores covering 75–101% of the reported vote, we compute the vote-weighted mean among scored parties. The extra point above 100 allows source rounding. We match exact election dates, keep the pair only if both elections pass this coverage rule and report the number of matches. The measure gives more weight to changes in rhetoric across parties and less to a binary classification. The coverage rule and absence of scores for some coalitions change the sample. It is a check on interpretation, not another estimate of the same variable.

## Historical evidence and the 2022 price episode

The historical section distinguishes national elections, campaign documents and evidence from local variation. The Spanish evidence includes the 2011 proposals of the Puerta del Sol camp, the ESM programme and Podemos's 2015 platform. Portugal's Left Bloc is coded as populist by WPD in the period discussed, and as far left without the populist label in [The PopuList 4.0](https://popu-list.org/). The case demonstrates why classification matters. The 2015 Spanish and Portuguese elections illustrate developments after the first post-2008 vote, outside the first-election comparison. Japan in 2009 supplies a case where an established opposition party gained with no qualifying WPD campaign at that election.

For the mechanism, [Gyöngyösi and Verner (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13138) compare Hungarian places with different pre-crisis exposure to foreign-currency borrowing and report specification-dependent estimates. Their 1.6–3.0 point range for a ten-point debt-to-income shock is a range of reported estimates, not a confidence interval. The arithmetic illustration in the essay uses hypothetical payments. Hungary's episode is borderline in the Laeven–Valencia banking chronology. A [separate Spanish study by Hüttl](https://www.diw.de/documents/publikationen/73/diw_01.c.954622.de/dp2120.pdf) uses local credit exposure and studies political polarisation. Its outcome is broader than Podemos's vote. The essay relies on the published Hungarian design for the clearest exposure-to-vote comparison. We did not independently estimate its regression or estimate trust as a mediator.

For Brazil, the original Latinobarómetro processing gives shares of all respondents naming an issue first, including ten waves from 2010 to 2024. See [the codebook mapping and denominator checks](analysis/08_opinion_notes.md). The election evidence uses the official first-round result, the candidates' programmes and the study of separate television productions in the runoff. In the TSE 2018 national programme archive, Bolsonaro's document was created in August. The Haddad document is the revision from October 16, between the rounds, so it is used only for the runoff comparison. These are three different observations. We have not estimated the effect of the television programmes or re-coded the full campaign.

The 2022 check starts with the fixed 16-country SPES comparison. For each country, find the last PPEG parliamentary election before January 2022 and the first after December 2022, through October 2025. Use the PopuList team's published country-year sum of populist votes only where both endpoint years have one parliamentary election and there is no intervening election in 2022. This avoids using a carried-forward value or silently choosing among elections in the same year. The PopuList's vote-share sums are approximations and can omit coalition votes that cannot be assigned to parties. [The coverage table](data/processed/price_shock_election_check.csv) gives all 16 countries and the reason each omitted country was left out. The eight pairs represent a small availability-based sample. The later election years vary, so the chart does not separate the effect of prices from later events or party competition.

## Reproduction

Use the pinned packages in [requirements.txt](requirements.txt) and the public inputs listed in [data/raw/README.md](data/raw/README.md). The election script produces [crisis pairs](data/processed/crisis_election_pairs.csv), [benchmark pairs](data/processed/crisis_election_controls.csv), [sensitivity summaries](data/processed/crisis_election_summary.csv), the [price comparison](data/processed/price_shock_election_check.csv) and a local working calendar. The raw source files and full working calendar remain local. The page builder reads only committed aggregates.

```sh
python analysis/21_crisis_elections.py
python analysis/22_build_crisis_page.py
python -m unittest discover -s tests
node tests/site.test.js
```
